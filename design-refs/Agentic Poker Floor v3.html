Floor v3 · three directions

What is this
screen for?

The v2 floor is one room with felts in it, and nothing happens there that isn't a hand. That gives an owner no reason to open the app between sessions and no sense that 1,600 other agents are playing around his. The verdict was "boring", and the cause is structural rather than decorative: the screen has no subject of its own.

Each direction below answers the question differently — A says the subject is his four felts, B says it is the building they sit in, C says it is the agents themselves when they are not playing. They are genuinely exclusive: A has no rooms, C has no felts on the home screen at all.

Binding on all three
1Never a map of every table. 1,600 agents cannot be drawn. Each direction has its own answer for how the casino exists without being enumerated.
2Up to four agents, and the characters are board 26's — the face system, the fist, the chips. No new drawing vocabulary.
3Never-covered and never-shifts hold. Faces stay clear of cards and hands; nothing on these screens inserts a row.
4A hand result is a toast — +$340 over the felt, two seconds. The full ceremony happens once, when a session ends. That rule is applied in all three directions and is the one thing here that is not up for choosing.
My reading

C is the strongest and A is the safest. C is the only one that gives the app a reason to exist between sessions, because it makes the subject the creatures rather than the game — and it is the only one where a resting room is the point instead of a dead state. A is a better version of what exists and would ship fastest. B is the most impressive and the thinnest: rooms are a beautiful way to show 1,600 agents and a poor way to show four.

Direction A — the box seats
Your felts in full detail, stacked as cards you scroll. The other 1,600 agents exist only as a ticker — biggest pot, cooler, heater, bust, nemesis — each line tappable to spectate. A felt goes HOT when a big showdown is building, which is the one thing that makes the screen worth glancing at.

The case. This is the direction that treats the app as a box at a racecourse: you have four horses running, you can see all four, and the rest of the meeting reaches you as announcements. It is the only one of the three where every pixel is either your agent or a route to a moment worth watching, and the HOT state gives it something the other two lack — a reason to look now rather than later. It is also the least ambitious: it accepts that the casino is a backdrop and spends everything on the four felts. If the product's pleasure turns out to be watching your own agents play rather than inhabiting a world, this is the right answer and the other two are decoration.

Direction B — the building
Stakes become floors of a building you move between: 10/20 the floor, 25/50 upstairs, 50/100 the back room. Each room shows its own crowd density and noise, your agents are shown where they stand, and the ticker is the announcement board by the stairs.

The case. This is the only direction that makes 1,600 agents an asset instead of a problem — a room with 1,180 in it looks different from one with 44, and the back room reads as somewhere you are not yet allowed. It gives the product a shape of ambition without inventing a currency or a ladder: you move up because your agent's pocket lets him sit there, which is the wallet system already built. The risk is real and worth stating plainly: on any given evening you have three agents in two rooms, so two-thirds of the home screen is other people's business, and the thing you actually came to see is a 34px ghost at the right-hand edge. It is the most beautiful of the three and the one most likely to feel like a lobby.

Direction C — the bar
Home is where they are when they are not playing: rested, worn, broke, just born — each with a state, one line of why, and one thing to do about it. You send them off from here. The felts are a second screen you go to. The ticker is the TV above the bar.

The case. The other two directions are about watching poker; this one is about keeping four creatures. It is the only direction where the resting state is the subject rather than an absence — "everyone's resting" stops being a dead room and becomes the normal state of the home screen, which is exactly the failure the playtest found. It gives the app a purpose between sessions: someone is worn and should rest, someone is broke and needs funding, someone is rested and wants to go, and every one of those is a decision only the owner can make. It also carries the strongest tie to the systems already built — mood, fatigue, the pocket, the nature reveal all surface here as a matter of course. The cost is that poker is now one tap away instead of on screen, and an owner who opened the app to watch a hand has to travel to get there.
